To reply to a customer message in another language, use the translation built into the app first: Instagram and Facebook can translate messages and comments, and TikTok can translate comments. Read the translation, write your reply in plain, short English, translate it back, and confirm the key details (date, time, price, address) in digits. For anything important, send the reply in both languages so nothing is lost.

This guide covers which languages UK small businesses are likely to see, the tools each platform gives you, and how to avoid the mix-ups machine translation causes.

## How common are messages in other languages?

More common than many owners expect, and very uneven by town. The [2021 Census language results from the ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/culturalidentity/language/bulletins/languageenglandandwales/census2021) found that 91.1% of people in England and Wales aged three and over had English (or Welsh in Wales) as their main language. The most common other main languages were:

| Language | People with it as their main language | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Polish | 612,000 | 1.1% |
| Romanian | 472,000 | 0.8% |
| Panjabi | 291,000 | 0.5% |
| Urdu | 270,000 | 0.5% |

The local picture varies a lot. The same ONS release found Polish as a main language ranged from 0.1% in Castle Point to 5.7% in Boston, Lincolnshire. A car valeter in Boston will see far more Polish messages than one in Castle Point.

Two things to bear in mind. "Main language" does not mean someone cannot read English; many people who write to you in Polish or Urdu read English perfectly well and are just writing the way they find quickest. And customers messaging on Instagram or Facebook often mix languages in one message, which trips up translation tools more than a single language does.

## What translation tools do Facebook, Instagram and TikTok have?

Each platform has something built in. None of them is perfect, and the exact menus change, so check the linked help pages if a button has moved.

| Platform | What it can translate | How |
|---|---|---|
| Instagram DMs | Messages you receive and send | Press and hold the message, tap **Translate**. [Added in 2025](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/02/new-instagram-dm-features-stay-connected/) |
| Messenger | Messages you receive | [Meta's help page on translating messages in Messenger](https://www.facebook.com/help/messenger-app/329901696451149) says it works on phones, not on computers |
| Messenger business chats | Both sides of a conversation, when a business turns it on | Meta describes [automatic translations in business chats](https://www.facebook.com/help/messenger-app/190534225983354): each side sees messages in its own language |
| Facebook posts and comments | Comments on your Page | **See translation** under the comment. [Facebook's help on translating posts and comments](https://www.facebook.com/help/509936952489634) |
| TikTok comments | Comments on your videos | Press and hold the comment and choose **Translate**. Your translation language is set in TikTok's language settings |

What these tools mostly do is help you *read*. Writing a reply back in Romanian is still up to you, unless you use automatic translation in a business chat or a separate tool.

## How do you reply in someone else's language without mistakes?

A routine that works for most small businesses:

1. **Translate their message and read it twice.** If anything looks odd ("I want to book my grandmother for a fringe"), assume the translation is wrong before you assume the customer is.
2. **Write your reply in short, plain English.** One idea per sentence. No idioms ("we are snowed under", "it is a doddle"), no slang, no jokes.
3. **Put numbers in digits and dates in full.** "Tuesday 12 January 2027 at 14:30, £45" translates cleanly. "Half two next Tuesday, forty-five quid" does not.
4. **Translate it, then translate it back.** Paste the translation into the tool again and turn it back into English. If the meaning has shifted, simplify your English and try again.
5. **Send both versions.** Their language first, then your English underneath. If the translation is clumsy, the English is there to check against.
6. **Confirm the important details back.** "To confirm: deep clean, 3-bed house, LE4 postcode, Monday 18 January, £140." Ask them to reply "yes".

### An example: a takeaway in Peterborough

A customer writes in Polish asking whether a dish contains nuts. The translation is clear enough. This is exactly the message not to answer from a quick translation alone, because a mistake could hurt someone.

A good reply, written in plain English and then translated:

> Thank you for asking. This dish is made in a kitchen that uses nuts. We cannot promise it is nut-free. Please call us before ordering so we can talk about your allergy.

Short sentences, no ambiguity, and a person takes over. The same goes for anything medical (salon treatments in pregnancy, for example), legal (contracts, deposits, cancellations) or about safety (gas, electrics).

## Where does machine translation go wrong?

| Problem | Example | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Names translated as words | A surname that is also a word gets turned into nonsense | Copy names exactly from the original message |
| Formal and informal "you" | Polish, Romanian and Urdu have more formal forms; a tool may pick the wrong one | A slightly formal reply is safer than an overfamiliar one |
| Slang and abbreviations | Text-speak in any language translates badly | Ask a simple clarifying question |
| Mixed languages | A message half in English, half in Panjabi | Translate the non-English part on its own |
| Script | Urdu is written right to left in its own script; Panjabi can be written in more than one script | Check the tool has detected the right language |
| Numbers and dates | 03/04 means 3 April in the UK and 4 March in the US | Write dates out in words and digits |

None of this needs a translator for everyday enquiries. It needs a bit of care and a habit of confirming details.

## What about data protection?

Customer messages are personal data under UK GDPR, and your business is the controller. Translation built into Instagram, Facebook and TikTok happens on the platform you are already using. If you copy messages into a separate translation website, check its terms: some free tools may keep what you paste. For a business, a tool with a data processing agreement is the safer choice, and your privacy notice should mention the software you use to handle messages.

## Is it worth replying in their language at all?

Usually, yes, for two practical reasons rather than politeness.

First, fewer misunderstandings. A customer who reads your reply in their own language is less likely to turn up on the wrong day.

Second, it is quicker for both of you. A worked example: say a cleaning company in Leicester gets 5 messages a week in another language, and each one currently takes 10 minutes of copying into a translator, working out a reply and checking it, against 3 minutes for an English message. That is 35 extra minutes a week. At an owner's time worth £30 to £50 an hour, that is about £18 to £29 a week. Not huge, but it is also the message most likely to be left until later, and later is often too late. ## Where to start

1. Look back at the last month of messages and count how many were in another language, and which ones.
2. Turn on translation in Instagram, Facebook and TikTok and try it on a real message.
3. Write your five most common answers in plain English, short sentences, numbers in digits. These translate well.
4. Decide which topics always go to a person: allergies, health, money, contracts, safety.
5. Get into the habit of confirming key details back, in both languages.
